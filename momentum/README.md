# Momentum on US large caps: 12-1 cross-sectional vs 50/200 crossover

Two momentum strategies on the same 190-name universe, the same trading clock and
the same cost model, so the comparison is about the signal and nothing else.

- **12-1 cross-sectional momentum.** Rank the eligible universe each month end by
  the return from t-252 to t-21, buy the top decile, short the bottom decile,
  equal weight within each leg. 200% gross, 0% net.
- **50/200 moving-average crossover.** Long each stock whose 50-day average is
  above its 200-day average, short the rest, each side equal weighted to 100%
  gross. Also run as a long-only variant, which is where it gets interesting.

The headline is negative: neither long-short strategy makes money on this
universe, in sample or out. I have kept the result rather than tuning until it
looked better, because the reason it fails is the most useful thing here.

## Design

The sample runs 2005-01-03 to 2026-08-28, with a warm-up from 2003-06 so the
252-day momentum window and the 200-day average are both complete on day one.

| Window | Dates | Use |
|---|---|---|
| In-sample | 2005-01-03 to 2020-12-31 | Build the pipeline, run diagnostics, walk-forward |
| Final test | 2021-01-01 to 2026-08-28 | Run once, at the end, untouched |

Baseline parameters were written down from the spec's Appendix A before any
result was looked at: 252/21 momentum, monthly rebalance, decile tails, equal
weight, one-day execution lag, 10bps one-way trading cost, 50bps/yr borrow.

**Look-ahead.** Signals are built from prices up to and including t and never
shifted in the signal code. The single lag lives in `backtest.run`, which shifts
the whole weight panel forward one day before it earns anything, so a book formed
on the close of t first sees the return of t+1. Both strategies inherit that lag
from the same line of code. `check.py` verifies it three ways: the weight earning
day t's return equals the weight set at t-1, a price spike at t leaves every
earlier signal byte-identical, and a deliberately leaked signal (ranking on the
return about to be earned) pushed through the same machinery scores Sharpe 17.1
where the real pipeline on the same data scores 1.77.

**Formation and evaluation never overlap.** The momentum score at a rebalance
date ends 21 trading days before that date, and the return it is graded on starts
the day after. The 21-day skip is a gap, not just a convention.

**Eligibility is point-in-time.** A name is tradable on date t only once it has
252 prints behind it and its trailing 63-day median dollar volume clears $10m.
The dollar-volume window ends at t-1. Names are filtered before ranking, so an
ineligible stock cannot move the decile cutoffs.

There is no minimum-price filter, and the first draft had one. The cached
closes are split-adjusted, so a $5 floor on them judges 2005 eligibility by
splits that happened years later: it removed NVDA for 3,495 stock-days, KLAC
for 2,612, LRCX and NFLX for over 2,300 each, AAPL and AMZN for over 1,000,
all in the years they were the biggest winners in the sample. That is
look-ahead in the universe rather than in the signal, and it cost the in-sample
long leg about three points a year. Dollar volume is split-invariant, so it
stays.

The out-of-sample result is marginally better than in-sample, which is the
opposite of the usual overfitting story and mostly says the in-sample number was
not a fitted number to begin with. A gross Sharpe of 0.19 on 68 months is not
distinguishable from zero.

### Signal quality

The in-sample information coefficient (Spearman rank correlation between the
momentum score and the next month's return) is **-0.0012 with a t-stat of -0.07**
over 192 months, positive in 54% of them. That is zero. The top-minus-bottom
decile spread on the same dates is -0.02% a month, t-stat -0.04, also zero.

Average next-month return by momentum quintile, in-sample:

| Quintile (1 = worst momentum) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean next-month return | +1.53% | +1.26% | +1.24% | +1.10% | +1.37% |

There is no monotone ordering. Two things stand out in this table. The slope is
slightly the *wrong* way, and every bucket earns roughly +1.2 to +1.5% a month,
15 to 18%/yr, including the worst-momentum bucket. A universe where the biggest
losers still compound at 18%/yr is not a fair sample, which is the next section.

### Walk-forward

Six configurations (126/189/252-day lookback, 10%/20% tails), one selected per
calendar year 2010-2020 using only the years before it, then applied to that year
untouched. The selection picked 12-1 with 10% tails in all eleven blocks and
never switched, because every candidate's training Sharpe was negative and it was
choosing among losers. The chained walk-forward stream returns **-1.33%/yr net,
Sharpe 0.06**. Because the walk-forward pick and the frozen baseline turned out
to be the same specification, the final test is one number, not two.

At 6.6x annual turnover the base assumption costs 1.82%/yr. The strategy has no
gross edge to spend, so cost is not what breaks it, but it does mean any future
version needs a gross Sharpe well above 0.2 before it is worth trading.

### 2020

Momentum's famous failure mode is the sharp reversal, and 2020 delivered a clean
one. The full year looks harmless (gross +5.62%, net +3.79%, against SPY
+18.33%). The path does not.

| Month | Momentum net | Long leg | Short leg | SPY |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2020-03 | +29.61% | -10.67% | +31.07% | -12.49% |
| 2020-04 | -11.51% | +14.88% | -24.02% | +12.70% |
| 2020-11 | **-23.65%** | +10.57% | **-30.41%** | +10.88% |

March was momentum's best month in the sample and it came almost entirely from
the short leg: the crash sold the prior losers hardest, so a book short the
bottom decile made 31% while its long leg lost 10.7%. Then the rebound reversed
it. April cost 24% on the short leg, and November cost 30.4%.

The worst day is 2020-11-09, the Pfizer vaccine announcement: **-20.79% in a
single session**, of which -16.79% was the short leg, on a day SPY rose 1.26%.
The names the strategy was short were the beaten-down travel, energy and
financial stocks, and they all gapped up together. Four of the five worst days of
2020 were days SPY rose.

Peak to trough from 2020 into the recovery, the strategy lost **-50.40%**,
bottoming on 2021-03-08.

This is the textbook momentum crash and it is a property of the strategy, not of
this implementation. The short leg is short high-beta distressed names after a
selloff, so it is implicitly short a violent recovery. Volatility scaling or beta
neutralisation is the standard defence and neither is implemented here.

### Survivorship

This is the part that matters most for interpreting everything above.

The universe is today's S&P 500 membership applied backwards to 2005. yfinance
has no point-in-time constituent history, so every name deleted from the index
between 2005 and now is simply absent. Four tickers in my own list
(BK, HES, IPG, MMC) no longer resolve on Yahoo at all, which is the bias visible
in miniature. 19 of the 190 names had no price on 2005-01-03 and enter later as
they list, which the eligibility flag handles correctly.

The level of the bias on this slice: the equal-weight eligible universe, with
membership decided at the close of t-1 and earning the return of t, returns
**+15.36%/yr** over 2005-2026 against SPY's **+10.94%/yr**, a gap of
**+4.42%/yr**. A basket chosen for being in the index today beats the index the
index actually was.

The first version of this README argued the sign of the bias on a long-short
book was unknown, because the short leg is starved of the deleted losers while
the long leg is fed by names that joined after winning. The `pit-universe`
project settled that by rebuilding S&P 500 membership from Wikipedia's change
list and dated list snapshots and running this project's `momentum.py`,
`backtest.py` and `data.eligibility` on the full index both ways, with the same
dates, rules and costs. Measured bias, today's members minus point-in-time:

| | In-sample 2005-2020 | Final test 2021-2026 | Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross annual return | +2.17%/yr | +6.35%/yr | +3.18%/yr |
| Net annual return | +2.13%/yr | +6.29%/yr | +3.14%/yr |
| Long leg | +8.44%/yr | +12.44%/yr | +9.43%/yr |
| Short leg | -3.84%/yr | -3.39%/yr | -3.73%/yr |

The bias is positive and it comes from the long leg. The short-leg half of the
old argument is real: the short leg is 3.4 to 3.8%/yr *better* on the
point-in-time universe, and in-sample a third of the point-in-time short book
sits in names no longer in the index. But the long leg swings 8 to 12%/yr the other way
and wins, because a long leg built from today's members is buying the top
decile of a list assembled by looking at who won. Point-in-time 12-1 momentum on
the full index is worse than the survivor version, not better: -8.4%/yr gross
in-sample, a -91% drawdown, Sharpe indistinguishable from zero in every window.
A fair universe makes the null result more null. These numbers are on the full
index, not the 190-name slice here, and the slice is a subset of today's
members that carries the same bias.

Two caveats on the measurement. It is a lower bound, because 257 of the 390
names ever removed from the index have no Yahoo prices at all, and those are
the bankruptcies and takeouts the short leg wants most. And the equal-weight
level of the bias on the full index is +3.97%/yr in-sample and +4.23%/yr in
the final test, of which about 3.2 points is holding future members before
they joined and 0.7 points is the deleted losers, so most of what the old
"survivorship" caveat described is really a look-ahead in the membership list.
The quintile table above, where the worst-momentum bucket still earns 18%/yr,
is what the missing deletions look like from inside the slice.

## Why it fails

Three things, in order of how much they matter.

1. **The universe is wrong for the signal.** Cross-sectional momentum is
   documented on broad universes including small and mid caps. 190 mega-caps give
   18 names per decile, and large caps are the segment where momentum is weakest
   and most arbitraged. The IC of -0.001 is a real measurement on this universe,
   not a claim about momentum generally.
2. **The universe flatters the result, by a measured +2.2%/yr gross in-sample
   and +6.3%/yr in the final test.** See above. The short leg is starved of
   deleted losers, but the long leg gains more from future members than the
   short leg loses, and the point-in-time version is worse.
3. **The crash risk is unhedged.** A dollar-neutral book at 25-27% vol with a
   -50% drawdown around one reversal has a return distribution that no Sharpe
   ratio summarises usefully.

The one strategy that does work is the least interesting one: 50/200 long only,
net +12.58%/yr in-sample and +14.02%/yr out-of-sample at Sharpe 1.02. That is
almost entirely equity beta on a survivor basket, and it beats SPY's +10.94%
by less than the +4.42%/yr selection gap on this slice (measured at +3.97 to
+4.23%/yr on the full index). I report it because it is the honest reference
point, not because it is a strategy.

## How to run

```
../.venv/bin/python3 check.py
../.venv/bin/python3 run.py
```

`check.py` is fully offline. `run.py` downloads once and caches to
`reports/prices.csv` and `reports/volumes.csv`, so reruns are offline too. Delete
those two files to refresh. No dependencies beyond what the shared
`requirements.txt` already installs.

`data.get_panel(universe="pit")` returns the same five objects built from the
point-in-time S&P 500 panel in `../pit-universe`: every name that was ever a
member, eligible only while it was one. The default stays `"today"`, so `run.py`
reproduces the numbers above unchanged. The two-universe comparison itself is
`../pit-universe/survivorship.py`.

## Limitations

The ones I would raise first if someone else handed me this.

**The headline numbers are on today's members.** Discussed at length above.
The bias is now measured rather than guessed, +2.2%/yr gross in-sample and
+6.3%/yr in the final test, and `data.get_panel(universe="pit")` loads the
point-in-time panel so the strategy can be run on it here. What no public
source fixes is the 257 removed names Yahoo no longer serves, so even the
point-in-time number is a lower bound; closing that needs CRSP with delisting
returns.

**Weights are held constant between rebalances.** The engine holds the target
weights flat for the month rather than letting them drift with prices. Real
positions drift, so the true book would need small daily trades to stay at target
and my turnover of 6.6x is therefore an underestimate. The direction of that
error is against the strategy, and at these Sharpes it does not change any
conclusion.

**No sector control.** The spec asks for a sector-aware variant and I did not
build one. Sector labels are loaded and carried through `data.py` but never used.
A decile book on 190 names can easily be long one sector and short another
without me measuring it, and given the 2020 result (short energy and travel into
a recovery) I would expect the sector exposure to be large. This is the first
thing I would add.

**Prices are split-adjusted only.** yfinance with `auto_adjust=True` returns one
adjusted close per name, so any rule that depends on the nominal price level (a
penny-stock floor, a tick-size cost model) cannot be applied honestly, and the
eligibility test is dollar volume and history only. On 190 S&P 500 names that
costs almost nothing; on a broader universe it would need unadjusted prices.

**Costs are a flat bps assumption.** No spread model, no market impact, no
participation limits, and short borrow is a single 50bps/yr rate. The stocks the
strategy shorts after a crash are exactly the ones that get expensive or
impossible to borrow, so a flat rate understates the cost of the short leg at
precisely the moments it matters.

**One universe, one market, one sample.** Every number here is 190 US large caps
over 21 years. Nothing tests whether the pipeline reaches the same conclusion on
mid caps, on another market, or on another period.

**The in-sample window contains 2008 and 2020.** Two regime breaks in sixteen
years means the in-sample estimates are dominated by two events, and the
per-year table shows how much single years swing the aggregate.
